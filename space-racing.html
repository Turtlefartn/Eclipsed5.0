Space Racing

The Zoom Zoom Thingy — Figure-8 Light-Sail Stack, and Racing With It: a figure-8 light-sail stack for leaving the solar system fast, and Rocket Races: a solar-system grand prix that uses orbits round each planet to reposition, re-aim, and go again.

Started 2026-10-11 as a max-speed question; became the Instagram “SOLVE THIS” challenge the same day. Racing section added 2026-10-24: make it useful for space racing too — orbits round each planet for repositioning whenever needed. Renamed “Space Racing” 2026-10-25 (was: Figure-8 Light-Sail Racing).

The number ledger — families never merge

Every figure on this page carries a label: flown flown actual   published published figure   derived calculated from vis-viva & patched conics   Fermi scenario estimate.

The challenge numbers and the 28,000-year numbers come from different runs and stay labelled as such.

Goals of the project — and the races

  1. 1. Push space the way racing pushed automobiles

    Auto racing was never just a show — it was the fastest development cycle the car industry ever had: disc brakes, carbon fibre, hybrid recovery systems, telemetry, the survival cell all came off racetracks and into road cars, because competition compresses a decade of cautious engineering into a season of desperate engineering. The races exist to do the same to spaceflight: sails, arrays, capture systems, pods, and life-support advancing at racing speed because somebody's standing depends on it this quarter, not next decade.

  2. 2. Push “us” out further, faster

    The races need infrastructure strung along every route — depots, brake beams, relay lenses, lifeboats, parking orbits — and once a race has been somewhere, that hardware stays there. Every circuit leaves the system more inhabited than it found it. The sport is the outward ratchet of the ladder: entertainment on the surface, permanent expansion underneath, each season's course longer than the last because last season's course is now serviced road.

  3. 3. It starts as semi-truck racing

    Honest framing: the first racers aren't sleek F1 machines — they're the freight hulls that already exist, racing each other at hauler pace on hauler margins. Semi-truck racing at least at the start. The sleek classes come later, once the trucks have built the road, the depots, and the audience.

Part 1 · 2026-10-11

The stack

The tools, and only these tools — no invented engines:

  1. Gravity assists — solar-system bodies only.
  2. Ion thrusters.
  3. Sunlight sails.
  4. Laser-pushed sails.

Former #5 removed from this list 2026-10-25 on Matthew's order: the figure-8 is not a stack tool for racing — its job is to push probes out of the system at as fast a speed as possible, so it now lives in its own section outside the racing bits, at the end of this page (“Separate from the racing — the probe shot”). Matthew's clarification, same day: the figure-8 is just a “placeholder” for infinite speed — the 8 is the infinity loop (∞ on its side): hold the pattern, keep banking, the placeholder stands for “speed without a stated ceiling” until real mechanisms (stack legs, rings) say how fast it actually gets. Placeholder, not a physics claim — the derived ceiling in the probe section still stands.

What each leg actually buys

  • Gravity alone: sustained solar-escape excess caps at ~27–39 km/s with an optimised 100-year Jupiter ladder derived (flown reference: Voyager-class ~17 km/s flown). Momentary ceiling is 618 km/s = 0.206% c on a solar-surface graze (flown reference: Parker-like orbit, 190 km/s at 0.046 AU flown). So gravity alone never reaches even 0.25% c (749 km/s) — sustained or momentary — and 25% c / 50% c are impossible at any timescale: one best-case Jupiter kick delivers ~5.2×108 J/kg against 2.95×1015 J/kg needed for 0.25c, and once you're above solar escape at Jupiter (18.5 km/s) you leave and can't come back for more kicks.
  • The figure-8 banks energy only up to escape. A bound pattern can hold at most 444 (km/s)² from an Earth-like orbit, 1,143 (km/s)² from a Parker-like orbit derived — the lap it crosses escape energy, it exits. It is a savings account with a hard ceiling, not an engine.
  • Ion alone is killed by the rocket equation at any timescale (NEXT Isp 4,100 s published: mass ratio 108 for 749 km/s; 10810 for 0.25c). The one rescue: per-lap refuelling at a depot riding the figure-8 — mass ratio ~2 per lap (~27.9 km/s per lap derived), which converts an exponential problem into a linear logistics problem.
  • The laser sail is the only leg that escapes the exponent — F = 2P/c, no onboard propellant. Worked figure derived: a tonne-class craft under a 1 GW laser at ~10% figure-8 duty cycle reaches 0.25% c in ~36 years, 0.50% c in ~71 years. 25–50% c survives only for gram–kilo Starshot-class craft, and only as a straight shot — never as a held pattern.

The 28,000-year verdict

Over the full Journey ladder horizon, the binding constraint is infrastructure arrival time, not physics push time. The figure-8 supplies only 0.16% of the 0.25% c target's energy (0.00004% of the 25% c target's) — a long hold is mostly parking while bigger lasers and relays get built Fermi (build-out scenario: GW array ~10–100 yr, 100 GW ~100 yr, 1 TW ~1,000 yr, 1 PW ~1,000–5,000 yr).

Part 2 · added 2026-10-24

Space racing: orbits round each planet as the repositioning toolkit

Rocket Races (2026-10-09): a solar-system grand prix — full circuit past every planet, fastest lap wins.

Part 1 is built for one job: leave, fast, once. A circuit demands the opposite skill set — turn around, re-aim, wait for geometry, and do it again at every planet, on a course that won't sit still because every planet is moving. That's what planetary orbits are for. An orbit round a planet isn't a detour off the course — it's the racer's pit lane, steering wheel, and waiting room in one.

Why a racing circuit at all — the usefulness argument

The escape stack still needs its basics: depots, relay lasers, capture corridors, sail handling at scale. None of that gets built for a vehicle that flies once a generation. The circuit is how the basics earn their keep. A racing season flies the same depots, relays, and aerocapture corridors thousands of times — stress-testing the exact infrastructure a ladder ship will later trust its life to, and giving every piece of it a paying, public, fun reason to exist in the meantime. Racing isn't a distraction from the serious stack; it's the serious stack's training ground and funding engine, and the escape capability rides free on top.

And the circuit changes all the time — that's a feature, not the hole it first looks like. Planetary configurations never repeat on human timescales, so windows open and close, legs lengthen and shorten, and last season's perfect line is this season's trap. A course that won't sit still can't be memorised or over-optimised; it can only be flown, which selects for exactly the general capability the ladder needs — repositioning, re-aiming, waiting well, turning hard — over single-trick speed. More configurations also means more classes, more formats, more excuses to fly: more fun things to do is a design requirement, not decoration, because fun is what keeps the calendar full and the infrastructure exercised between the rare serious launches.

The seven jobs an orbit does for a racer

1. Waiting room (phasing)

Planets won't phase for you. Captured into orbit at a planet, you hold position in its gravity well — no drift, no station-keeping burn worth naming — and depart on the exact vector the moment the next leg's transfer window opens. The orbit is how a craft with feeble thrust waits precisely.

2. The hard turn (gravity steering)

A flyby bends your velocity vector for free; entering orbit, riding part of a revolution, and exiting gives you the largest heading changes in the sport at zero propellant. Under thrust, a big plane change at racing speed is ruinously expensive; timed right, the planet does it as a gift. Jupiter is the great steering wheel of the outer system; Venus and Earth run the inner circuit.

3. Oberth laps

Any burn is worth most at periapsis, deep in the well. An orbit hands you a scheduled periapsis pass every revolution — ion thrust stacks lap over lap at the best point, the same trick as the figure-8's sun-dive, miniaturised at every planet.

4. Aerocapture — the sail craft's only brakes

A sail has no reverse gear worth the name, and an ion drive can't shed arrival speed in time. Dipping into an atmosphere (Venus, Earth, Mars thinly, Titan, the gas giants) sheds the speed and drops you into orbit without propellant. For sail classes this is the only cheap way to stop at a planet at all — which makes it the move that unlocks every other job on this list.

5. Sail geometry reset

A sunlight sail's thrust direction is set by its angle to the Sun — mid-course, the vectors you can push along are rationed. Parked in orbit, you're carried around the planet while the planet carries you around the Sun, so your departure asymptote and sun-angle sweep through options for free. Wait half a revolution and the geometry you needed comes to you. The laser version: a relay/laser station in orbit round the planet is a boost node for the next leg — the focusing-distance wall from Part 1, dismantled one planet at a time.

6. Depot stops

Orbits are where the infrastructure lives: ion propellant, spare sails, repair. The figure-8's per-lap refuelling rescue generalises — every planet's orbit is a depot node, and the race becomes a logistics contest as much as a piloting one.

7. Local figure-8s

The sun–Jupiter 8 is one instance of a pattern. Resonant loops — planet-and-moons, planet-and-planet — give a racer a repeating slalom that returns them to the same gate every period, banking energy and repositioning on a timetable instead of by improvisation.

The ships live up there

The racers themselves are orbit-native: the ships stay in orbit, waiting for their teams to board — Star Wars style, crews ride pods up, dock, and board; the ship never lands. Turnaround is a pod schedule, not a surface launch window. This isn't just flavour — it's the only arrangement the stack makes sense in: the big sail rigs are fragile and enormous, laser departures start from orbit anyway, and hauling a racer down a gravity well and back up for every event would tax it for a trip it gains nothing from. The parking orbit at the depot node is the ship's home and its garage; the paddock, the team, and the crowd infrastructure hang around it.

Holes, kept honest: the pod ferry is a permanent per-event transport tax plus a boarding-safety problem that never goes away; a parked fleet eats micrometeorite exposure the whole time it waits; and once every team parks at the same fashionable node, parking orbits become a traffic-management problem of their own.

Where the crews live: ISS first, the Moon when it's good

Surface launches scrub — weather, range conflicts, a sensor having a bad day — and a racing calendar can't be held hostage to Florida skies. So the human side stages in orbit too: keep the ISS alive and add race crew quarters to it — crews live there and “launch” from the ISS instead of from Earth, podding out to their parked ships when their window opens. A race start becomes an orbital departure, which doesn't care about weather; the scrub problem shrinks to beam windows and ferry schedules, both of which are plannable.

This holds at least until the Moon is good — once lunar staging exists (the D-Mom 6.0 thread: Moon as shipyard and gas station), crew quarters and the race village move there, where the gravity is shallow, the sky is never cloudy, and departures are cheap.

Honest holes: the ISS flies at a 51.6° inclination, badly tilted against the ecliptic race course — early departures pay a plane-change tax (or early circuits are designed around it); the station is aging, so “keep it alive” means real refurbishment money and eventually a successor station inheriting the quarters role; and ISS-scale capacity houses a handful of crews, not a fleet — racing at scale needs the bigger depot-node station, with ISS as its proof of concept.

Victory parameters — stages from liftoff

Draft, 2026-10-24, for Matthew's word. No victory parameters exist yet; these are the proposed definitions to react to.

  • Liftoff = the clock start. In the orbit-native era, “liftoff” is the departure from the staging orbit: beam-on for laser classes, departure burn for the rest. Everything is timed from that instant.
  • The race is stages. Each leg between planetary gates is a stage, timed gate-to-gate. A racer must check every gate in order — a gate is checked by the class rule for that gate (orbit capture, or a flyby through the gate corridor) — and a missed gate voids the stage.
  • Two kinds of victory, both kept. Stage wins: fastest on an individual leg, recognised on their own (a team can have a bad circuit and still take stages). Overall victory: lowest total elapsed time, liftoff to final gate, across all stages — the grand prix title.
  • Records are era-stamped. Because the course moves, every record carries its planetary configuration / window. A record is “fastest under that alignment,” never an eternal number.
  • Classes stay separate. Sail, ion, laser-pushed, and mixed-stack racers do not share titles; within laser classes, records also note which arrays fired (Part 3) so a win can't quietly be an infrastructure gift.
  • Speed limit in the corners. Every gate has a posted maximum arrival speed, set per gate by the physics of that corner — the brake beam's capacity and the capture corridor's width. Arrive hot over the limit and the stage is penalised or void, exactly like speeding in an F1 pit lane; the gate satellites and the brake array's own tracking do the measuring (the cameras are the speed guns — nothing to argue with). It's a safety rule that doubles as the sport's core skill: the corner stops being about who carries the most speed and becomes a braking duel — who dares brake latest and still make the gate — which is where races get won by hands instead of hardware.
  • The scrub parameter (open — his call). If a beam window or ferry is scrubbed, does the clock start at the scheduled liftoff (pressure to go, scrub hurts) or the actual departure (scrub is free, only flying time counts)? Proposed default: actual departure for stage times, scheduled for overall — but this one changes racer behaviour more than any other line here, so it stays open until he rules.

The racer's rule: Orbit when you need to wait, turn hard, refuel, or re-aim a sail. Fly by when you only need a bend. Every capture costs time — arrive, settle, revolve, depart — so a stop only pays when the repositioning it buys beats the flyby's free rotation. Sprint classes may never capture once; grand prix and endurance classes will live in orbit half their race.

Honest holes — racing edition

  • Stopping is losing — sometimes. The whole sport is fastest lap; every orbit insertion is a wager that position beats momentum. Race formats will split on this: expect an argument forever between flyby purists and orbit strategists. That argument is the sport's tactics layer.
  • Sunlight dies with distance. Sail thrust falls as 1/r² — out past Jupiter a sunlight sail is nearly a passenger. Outer-system repositioning leans on gravity, ion, and pre-placed lasers; the sail classes are inner-system specialists unless the relay network exists.
  • A circuit needs lasers everywhere, pointing both ways. One Earth array boosts one direction. A lap of the system needs arrays or relay lenses at multiple planets, serviceable for departures in every leg's direction — Part 1's infrastructure wall again, wearing racing colours. First era of the sport is therefore probably inner-system, where one sun and a couple of arrays cover the course.
  • Aerocapture at racing speeds is a knife's edge. Arrival energy at Jupiter-class speeds makes capture corridors narrow and heating brutal — miss high, you skip out and lose the planet; miss low, the atmosphere keeps you. Gas-giant capture is the extreme case.
  • The course moves. “Fastest lap” needs a definition that survives orbital mechanics — a lap started in January and a lap started in June are different races. Classes and records have to be era-stamped (planetary configuration) or windowed (everyone launches in the same alignment), the way sailing records are weather-windowed.

Part 3 · added 2026-10-24

One laser family, many sizes: the near-c pusher, scaled for racing

The PW-class array that one day pushes the near-light-speed ship is the top of a family, not a one-off. Build the same system in multiple versions, sized down and stationed around the system, and the family spends the centuries between interstellar shots doing daily work: pushing racers around whatever circuit the planets are currently offering.

The versions

1. The Departure Array (interstellar class)

The original: PW-scale, built last, flown rarely — the near-c shot. Everything below is its proving line and its parts bin.

2. Grand-prix arrays (planetary class)

GW–TW arrays anchored at circuit planets — surface or orbital — that push racers onto the next leg. This is the leg engine of the full-circuit grand prix: leave Earth under Earth's array, get handed off, arrive at Mars, leave under Mars's array. No planet's array, no departure from that planet at racing pace.

3. Orbital boost arrays (mobile class)

Smaller arrays in planetary orbit that reposition themselves — riding the orbit round until they're lined up with the next leg, then firing. Part 2's orbit toolkit applied to the infrastructure itself: the start line moves to where the course needs it. These are the flexible, schedulable units that make ad-hoc circuits possible.

4. Relay lenses (the corner-workers)

Lightly powered or passive focusing relays stationed between planets. A circuit is not a straight line, and a laser only pushes straight — so the beam gets handed round corners and re-focused leg by leg. The relays are also the direct ancestors of the relay lenses the 1,000 t / 25% c case is conditional on in Part 1: racing builds them first, the interstellar shot inherits them proven.

5. Destination brake arrays (“break beams”)

A laser pushes — which means an array at the arrival planet, aimed back at an incoming sail, is a brake. Destination-side braking bleeds off racing speed before aerocapture, turning Part 2's knife-edge capture corridor into a managed approach. Push-only physics, used in both directions by standing at both ends.

In racing they're not optional kit, they're half the course: a gate only counts as a checking gate for sail and laser classes if a brake beam stands at it — a gate you can't slow down for is just a flyby with a flag on it. Stage finishes are therefore defined by brake-beam sites as much as by start arrays, and (per Part 5) the brake array's tracking optics are the arrival camera: every stage ends on film, lit by its own brake beam.

6. Shepherd beams (operations class)

Low-power precision arrays for start-line positioning, formation holding, final-approach nudges, and pushing a disabled racer clear of the course. Not propulsion in the heroic sense — race operations, tow truck and marshal in one.

The scheduling doctrine — circuits follow the course, not the calendar

The network doesn't run a fixed season. A circuit exists when the geometry is good: when a set of planets, arrays, and relay lines actually bear on each other, that circuit opens — an Earth–Mars sprint in one window, the full grand prix in a rare grand alignment, an inner-system slalom when the outer legs won't line up. Race control's real job is reading the ephemeris and tasking the shared array family to whichever course the solar system is offering this month. Off-season, the same arrays shuttle freight, service depots, and bank maintenance hours — and every firing, racing or freight, is one more proof-run for the day the Departure Array takes its one serious shot.

Honest holes — laser family edition

  • Hand-off is the hard part. Beam-riding stability and relay hand-off precision at racing distances are unproven at any size; a dropped hand-off mid-leg is a racer coasting off-course. Expect the early sport to be short legs between close relays, growing longer as hand-off matures.
  • Sail heating caps sprint power. Part 1's sail-survival wall applies per class: a sprint array can out-power what a small sail can survive absorbing. Classes will be defined as much by sail thermal limits as by array output.
  • Brake arrays invert the build order. Destination braking needs the far end built first — so circuits grow outward from the inner system, where both ends of a leg exist early. Outer-system racing waits on outer-system arrays, same infra-gating as everything else in this file.
  • One network, many masters. Racing, freight, and the rare interstellar campaign all draw on the same arrays. Scheduling priority — who gets the big arrays when windows collide — is a governance problem wearing a sports costume, and it will be fought over exactly like launch windows are now.

Part 4 · added 2026-10-24

F1 in space: management structure, rules, committees

Rocket Races gets run the way Formula 1 is run — because F1 already solved the problem of a dangerous, ruinously expensive, technology-driven sport staying a sport instead of an arms race with a finish line.

Governing principle: the teams run it

All of this — the federation, the commercial group, the committees, the circuits — is set up to be run and managed by the teams. They found it, they fund it through the Compact, they staff the committees, and they can rewrite the whole settlement on the Compact cycle. The bodies below are the teams' instruments, not their bosses: there is no outside owner of the sport. (Which is exactly why Part 4's capture hole matters — a team-run sport's chronic disease is the biggest teams bending it, and the Compact cycle plus the crews' independent seat are the standing counterweights.)

The two halves — F1's core split, kept

  • The Federation (the FIA seat): owns the rulebook and the safety case. Sanctions events, licenses people and ships, runs the committees below. It does not sell anything.
  • The Commercial Group (the F1 Group / FOM seat): owns the calendar, the broadcast, the sponsorship, the money. This is the funding engine of Part 2 made an institution — the commercial side's revenue is what pays for depots, relays, and array time between seasons.
  • The Compact (the Concorde Agreement seat): the contract binding federation, commercial group, and teams — who gets paid what, who commits to show up to which circuits, how rules change. Renegotiated on a cycle; every fight worth having happens there, on paper, instead of on the course.

The committees

1. Sporting Committee

Race formats, stage definitions, the victory parameters of Part 2 (they live here once ratified), gate rules, penalties, the calendar as sport: which circuits run, in which windows. (The open scrub-clock parameter lands here for a ruling.)

2. Technical Committee

The class rulebook: what a sail class is, array power limits per class, ion propellant specs, ship safety cell, telemetry black-box standard. Runs homologation and scrutineering: no ship races a spec it hasn't proven on the bench and in inspection.

3. Safety Committee

Capture-corridor standards, aerocapture envelopes, pod/boarding procedure, orbital-debris rules for parking orbits, rescue doctrine (the shepherd beams of Part 3 are its marshal service). Holds the sport's one absolute veto: it can stop any event, any stage, any ship, no appeal on the day.

Escape pods are mandatory, no exceptions: every crewed element — racing ship, crew quarters, depot station — carries escape pods rated for its full crew, inspected at scrutineering, with pod drills part of crew licensing; a ship or station short a working pod does not board crew, full stop.

And the pods are never idle, and never cold: they sit at launch readiness at a moment's notice — armed, provisioned, and undelayed, because a pod that needs preparing during the emergency is decoration, not equipment.

Each pod is also always processing how to get its escapee back to Earth as fast as possible — a live return solution recomputed continuously against the ship's current position, velocity, and the moving planets, so the instant a pod fires it is already flying the fastest Earth-return plan that exists at that moment, not improvising one.

Honest limit the processing itself has to respect: from the outer system, even the fastest Earth return can exceed a small pod's endurance — so the same always-on planning carries the divert case: if Earth can't be reached alive, the pod's fastest survivable plan (nearest depot, station, or crewed ship) is the one it flies, and it says so plainly to the escapee rather than dying on a technicality.

That divert case is why the system also keeps lifeboats scattered around — uncrewed, long-endurance refuge craft pre-positioned at gates and depots and spaced along the long legs, funded under the Compact as course infrastructure. A lifeboat is the pod's real destination at distance: bigger air, power, and legs than any pod, able to hold survivors, rendezvous with incoming pods, and make the slow trip home or to a depot that a pod never could.

Their spacing sets the sport's deepest safety rule: no point on a sanctioned course is ever beyond a pod's reach of a lifeboat, a depot, or a crewed haven — if a proposed circuit has a gap, the circuit doesn't run until a lifeboat is parked in it.

4. Array & Infrastructure Committee

Part 3's governance problem given a desk: scheduling the shared laser family across racing, freight, and maintenance; priority rules when windows collide; publishing beam-time allocations so a win is never a quiet infrastructure gift.

5. Stewards' Panel + Court of Appeal

Stewards (per event) rule on incidents, gate disputes, and protests during the event; the Court of Appeal (standing) hears appeals after. Deliberately separate people from the committees that wrote the rules.

6. Teams' Association & Crews' Association

The FOTA / GPDA seats — the teams negotiate the Compact collectively; the crews hold their own body for safety and working-conditions voice, because the people inside the ships get a seat the money can't buy out.

The rules, in three books — F1's structure

  • Sporting Regulations — how races run: stages, gates, victories, holds, protests, points.
  • Technical Regulations — what may race: classes, dimensions, power, materials, inspection.
  • Financial Regulations — the cost cap, translated: because the pay-to-win failure mode here isn't a richer team, it's a team that books all the beam-time. Array access is therefore allocated and capped by the Infrastructure Committee, not simply sold — beam-hours per team per season, tradable only under committee rules. The cap is what keeps the sport a contest of flying instead of accounting.

Licensing

  • Racer's licence (the super licence seat): graded by class — you earn your way up from shepherd-beam tugs and inner-system sprints to grand-prix arrays; nobody's first race is a TW beam.
  • Team licence & ship homologation: a team is licensed as an organisation (safety record, finances, facilities — including its parking-orbit and depot arrangements); each ship is homologated per class per season.

Honest holes — governance edition

  • Jurisdiction. F1's federation leans on national law underneath it. In orbit there is no underneath — a racing federation ends up writing de-facto space law (registration, liability, rescue obligation) whether it wants to or not. That makes this body a proto-authority over the same infrastructure the ladder depends on — a feature if designed for it, a capture risk if not.
  • Regulator capture is F1's own chronic disease — the biggest teams bend rulebooks across decades. The Compact's renegotiation cycle and the crews' independent seat are the counterweights on paper; they work only as long as someone uses them.
  • The safety veto vs the show. The one fight F1 has every decade: safety says stop, commerce says run. Putting the veto beyond same-day appeal settles who wins; expect it to be tested the first time a grand-alignment grand prix — a once-in-decades course — gets weather of the solar kind.

Part 5 · added 2026-10-24

The show around the sport: film & series about the teams, builds, ships, drivers

Formula 1's modern boom wasn't won on track — it was won by a documentary series that made the garage the drama. Rocket Races builds that layer in from the start, because here the media layer does four jobs at once:

  • The build shows. Series following teams through design, build, and scrutineering — the ship as a character under construction, engineering decisions as plot. This is also the sport's best recruitment film: every build show is an advertisement for the licence ladder and the trades behind it (the Department of Magical MisStories thread — curriculum wearing a story's clothes).
  • The season documentary (the Drive to Survive seat). Drivers, team bosses, array operators, rescue crews — rivalries, failures, the scrub that cost a season. Personalities are what convert a spectator into a fan, and fans are what the Commercial Group sells. The drivers are the face; the build crews and beam operators are the depth.
  • The race broadcast itself is a production craft. Racing ships are points of light at astronomical distance — there is no human trackside. The trackside camera sits at the beam location: the array has to track the sail precisely to push it, so the array's own tracking optics are already the best camera on the leg — and a sail under the beam is lit up brighter than anything else in the sky, a self-illuminating shot no other sport gets for free. Around that spine, camera satellites stationed at gates, relays, and depots cover the captures, turns, and orbit work the beams never see, with telemetry rendering filling the long dark between events. Whoever masters this constructed broadcast owns how the sport looks, which is a Commercial Group asset class of its own.
  • The record. Era-stamped records (Part 2) need receipts: the film archive is the verification layer — configurations, beam logs, and gate footage preserved as the sport's memory, so a record from a past alignment can be audited decades later.

Why it's structural, not decoration: media revenue flows into the Compact (Part 4) and out again as depot and array funding (Part 2's engine); cameras in the build shops double as informal scrutineering — a team that knows the world is watching builds honest ships; and the archive is the only reason “fastest under that alignment” means anything to anyone who wasn't there.

Honest holes — the show edition

  • Documentary editing manufactures rivalries and villains (the Drive to Survive sin — real people edited into characters, with real consequences in the paddock).
  • Drama pressure pushes against the Safety Committee's veto from a new direction — a camera loves a risky capture, and an audience is a lobby.
  • Build-shop access is a competitive leak, so teams will stage-manage what the cameras see, which quietly degrades the informal-scrutineering benefit unless access terms are written into the Compact.

Part 6 · added 2026-10-24

Races as the deployment railway: launching “goals” along the route

The racing calendar is also the system's deployment schedule. When something comes up that needs to be put somewhere along a route — a lifeboat into a newly opened gap (Part 4's rule), a replacement relay lens, a sensor, rescue gear, a probe — a race already going that way carries and deploys it as part of the event. In Matthew's word, the races are used to launch “goals”: deployable objectives that ride the sport because the sport is already moving.

The arithmetic is the appeal: capacity multiplies by the number of teams. Every team is a hull traversing the route anyway, so an N-team event is N deployment opportunities along that route, and the calendar's frequency becomes the system's deployment cadence — no dedicated launch campaign, no waiting for a bespoke mission. The hulls exist because racing justifies them; the deployments ride free.

Working rules, sketched for the committees: deployment duty is part of the team licence (Part 4) — a team that races the route can be tasked on it; emergency goals (a lifeboat for a gap, rescue assets toward an incident) pre-empt a team's race plan under Safety Committee order, with the Compact making the team whole for the spoiled event; routine goals (lenses, sensors, depot freight) are scheduled by the Infrastructure Committee and credited in the Compact's accounts. A race in progress is also, not incidentally, the nearest heavy traffic to any incident on its route — the deployment railway doubles as the first-response network.

Hole, kept

A deployment that changes a ship's mass or profile mid-event touches the fairness of the stage it's racing — deployment-carrying stages need their own scoring treatment (the Sporting Committee's problem), and a goal that can't wait for a race still needs the dedicated launch it was always going to need. The railway is the default, not the only road.

Part 7 · added 2026-10-24

Getting it off the ground: the bootstrap path

Four rungs, each leaving the hardware and habits the next one stands on.

  1. 0

    Rung 0 — Paper (done)

    The source document is the rulebook draft; the Instagram “SOLVE THIS” challenge (Part 1) is the public seed, and its terms are the bootstrap: four years of entrants building calcs/models, then the 100 fastest fly. The first races are races between models.

  2. 1

    Rung 1 — The simulation league

    Race the models properly: real ephemeris, the real victory parameters (stages, gates, era-stamps, corner speed limits) inside orbital simulators. The rulebook gets debugged for free, the first teams form around entries, and the Part 4 committees begin as hats worn by the same five people. Near-zero cost; buys community, formats, and a tested rulebook.

  3. 2

    Rung 2 — Small real hardware

    The sail leg is already flown history (IKAROS; LightSail 2, which raised its orbit on sunlight alone; NASA's ACS3 composite sail) flown. First physical classes: gram/kilo sailcraft in Earth orbit and cislunar space racing orbit-raising and course-keeping legs, with a modest ground laser providing the first real start line and brake beam. Semi-truck racing as specced — except the first trucks are cubesats.

  4. 3

    Rung 3 — Cislunar circuits, then outward

    Moon-round courses once staging exists there; the ISS crew-quarters era (Part 2) overlaps this rung in LEO and teaches the boarding, pod, and lifeboat drills cheap, before anyone needs them at Mars distance.

The thread tying the rungs together is the F1 lesson from Part 5: media from rung 1, not rung 3. Build shows and the season documentary can follow sim teams and cubesat shops long before a grand prix exists — audience first, sponsorship second, hardware third. That is the order that got automobiles their century of racing-driven advancement, and it is Goal 1 of this project run as a schedule.

Part 8 · added 2026-10-25

The founding teams

No teams have signed yet — these are the ten founding slots for the premier Semi-Truck class (refit freight hulls, mixed stack, Part 4 team licence + ship homologation applies to all of them). Each slot is an archetype a real team takes, names, and makes their own when they licence in. The point of ten different slots is ten different ways to win: a grid where everyone runs the same strategy is a parade, not a sport.

1. Semiworks Hauler Prime — the original truck

A straight refit freight hull, mixed ion + sail, Earth depot home. Orbit strategists: capture at every gate, refuel on schedule, never be clever. Wins on not breaking.

Hole: slowest raw pace on the grid — they need other teams to make mistakes.

2. Sundive Syndicate — the figure-8 specialists

Tonne-class laser sail built around the sun-dive. Flyby purists: bank energy lap after lap, never capture, release at full bank.

Hole: sail heating is their ceiling, and one bad graze ends a season, not a stage.

3. Brake Late Racing — the braking duelists

Freight hull tuned for aerocapture and brake-beam approaches. Their whole game is Part 2's corner rule: arrive at the posted gate limit, brake last, make the gate anyway.

Hole: the speed limit is also their whole personality — penalties and voided stages are a permanent line in their budget.

4. Big Bend — the gravity team

Outer-system specialists who treat Jupiter as the great steering wheel (Part 2, job 2). Minimal thrust, maximum line: the planet does the turning for free.

Hole: their course only exists when the planets line up — in bad alignments they're a midfield team waiting for geometry.

5. Pit Lane Orbital — the logistics team

Depot kings. Per-lap ion refuelling (Part 1's rescue, run as a sport) and depot-stop timing rehearsed like pit stops. They win races in the stops nobody broadcasts.

Hole: entirely dependent on depot and array scheduling — the Infrastructure Committee's calendar is their real opponent.

6. Relay Runners — the deployment-railway team

The Part 6 specialists: they carry and deploy “goals” — lifeboats, relay lenses, sensors — better than anyone, and double as the grid's shepherd-beam / first-response crew. Fastest in messy, incident-filled races.

Hole: deployment-carrying stages still need their own scoring treatment (Part 6 hole) — until the Sporting Committee rules, their best work may not count cleanly.

7. Feather Freight — the lightweight sail team

Smallest legal hull, largest sail area per tonne, laser-class reflexes on a truck-class entry. Devastating in the inner system where sunlight and arrays are strong.

Hole: past Jupiter the sunlight dies as 1/r² (Part 2 hole) — out there they're passengers with a nice sail.

8. Station Crew Racing — the ops team

The ISS-era crew (Part 2): fastest pod boarding, fastest turnaround, ships parked and crewed like a station watch rotation. They win the calendar, not the lap — more race-ready days than anyone.

Hole: older staging and the ISS's 51.6° inclination tax until the Moon staging era replaces it.

9. Sovereign Circuit — the state-backed slot

The archetype for a sovereign programme (the Saudi / Xi pattern): national array time, new hardware every season, a circuit as a prestige project.

Hole: the Financial Regulations were written for them — beam-hours are allocated and capped (Part 4), so money buys a team, not the arrays.

10. Works Team One — the billionaire works slot

The archetype for a private inventor-owner backer (the Stark / Luthor pattern): driver-engineer-owner, radical hull ideas a season early, everything built in-house.

Hole: governance capture is the standing risk (Part 4 hole) — the Compact and the crews' independent seat exist because this slot, specifically, will test them.

Grid rules that keep ten teams a sport: beam-hours capped and allocated per team per season (Part 4 Financial Regulations); every team carries deployment duty under its licence (Part 6); no team races short a working escape pod, ever (Part 4 Safety Committee); a slot is a licence, not property — a team that stops showing up loses the slot to the next licensed entrant under the Compact.

Separate from the racing · moved out of the racing bits 2026-10-25

Separate from the racing — the probe shot: push probes out of the system as fast as possible

This is not a race and not part of the racing programme — it gets its own section, outside the racing bits, because its job is the opposite of a circuit's: one way, out of the solar system, at the highest speed possible, and never come back. No gates, no captures, no corner speed limits, no return leg. A probe doesn't need brakes, a crew, escape pods, or a finish line — it needs every leg of the stack pointed the same way, once.

#5 itself — the figure-8: the placeholder for infinite speed

#5 is the fifth item under “The stack” (clarified by Matthew 2026-10-25) — and this section is where it lives now. The figure-8: an inner lobe that sun-dives (~0.05–0.1 AU) and an outer lobe out to Jupiter, held lap after lap until the speed is banked, then released. That release is the probe shot.

And in Matthew's words it is just a “placeholder” for infinite speed — the 8, ∞ on its side: the loop you draw when the honest answer is “as fast as possible, ceiling not yet named.” The placeholder marks the ambition; the mechanisms in this section (and their holes) mark what is actually claimed. Placeholder, not a physics claim — the derived ceiling stands right next to it:

Banking verdict derived (Part 1): a bound figure-8 can only hold energy up to escape — at most 444 (km/s)² from an Earth-like orbit, 1,143 (km/s)² from a Parker-like orbit — the lap it crosses escape energy, it exits. A savings account with a hard ceiling, spent in one shot on a probe.

What it uses

The rest of the Part 1 stack pointed at the same exit (gravity assists + ion + sunlight/laser sails), on top of #5 above — which is where this idea started on 2026-10-11, before racing existed. The verdicts there are its speed limits: the laser sail is the only leg that escapes the rocket-equation exponent; 0.25–0.50% c is infrastructure-gated, not physics-gated; relativistic speeds survive only for gram–kilo craft as a straight shot (all labelled in Part 1, families unmerged).

The rings: magnetic speed boosts, railgun-style

The planned “rings” are also used for possible magnetic speed boosts — railgun-style. A ring the probe passes through can hand it a magnetic push as it goes, ring after ring, on top of the sail/gravity/ion legs — one more boost leg that, like the laser, puts no propellant on the probe. Candidate primary home for the rings is here, on the probe shot, because a probe can take acceleration a crewed racer can't.

Honest holes — rings edition (not set)

  • Ring alignment and switching timing at probe speeds.
  • Power per ring vs boost actually delivered.
  • How many rings a route needs before the boost is worth the infrastructure.
  • Whether rings ever suit crewed racing at all (g-loads) or stay probe-only.

Ring design, placement, and count: not set.

Why it sits next to racing at all

Shared infrastructure, nothing more. The arrays, relays, depots, and rings the circuits build and exercise every season (Parts 2–3, 6) are the same hardware a probe shot fires from — racing keeps it maintained and proven between the rare serious launches. Compound accounting, again: the probe shot is a strongest-leg use of the stack; the sport rides alongside it, neither one is the other's reason.
