# Chapter 14 — The Dolls

She kept them on the glass shelf above the sink, where other women kept perfume. Eleven dolls, eleven selves, each in the outfit of the room she'd worn her in. The boardroom one with the sharp shoulders. The one for men who needed to feel interesting. The one for cameras, who smiled with exactly eight teeth because nine looked hungry and seven looked poor. The one for her mother, who still believed. The one for the afterparty, who could drink until the sky changed its mind. The one for 3 AM, who didn't have a face so much as a weather.

She dusted them every Sunday. That was the ritual nobody knew about — not the pills, not the parties, not the little blue ones she took before interviews so her hands wouldn't shake on camera. The dusting. You have to maintain yourselves, she'd say to the empty bathroom, or they maintain you.

Tonight she took one down. Held her up to the neon coming through the blinds, turning her slowly in the pink light. The camera one. Eight teeth. She'd worn this self for six years, through three shows and two scandals and one funeral she wasn't supposed to attend but did, standing in the back in sunglasses while the eight teeth stayed home in their case. "Which one of you," she asked the shelf, "is the one who leaves?"

None of them answered. They never do. That's the worst part — they're all her, and none of them know the way out.

The first doll she'd ever made was an accident. She was nineteen, new to the city, sleeping on a mattress that smelled like somebody else's decisions. A casting director told her she was "almost right, but too —" and then made a gesture with his hand like he was smoothing something invisible. Too what. Too herself. So she went home and practiced being less herself in the mirror until the mirror showed someone the gesture would approve of. That was doll number one. She didn't know it then. She thought she was just getting the job.

By twenty-four there were five of them and she had a system. Sunday nights, after the week had used her up, she'd sit on the bathroom tile in a towel and decide which self had earned a rest and which one had to work the week ahead. It felt like management. It felt like being good at something. Her therapist — the one she paid in cash and never told her agent about — said, "You talk about yourself like a staff." She fired the therapist. Hired a new one. Taught that one the system too, eventually. Everybody learned the system eventually. That was the problem with the system: it worked.

The pills came later, and they weren't the dolls. People got that wrong in the profiles. The dolls weren't the drugs — the dolls were the reason for the drugs. Each self had her own prescription. The boardroom one needed the white ones to be sharp. The afterparty one needed the red ones to be soft. The 3 AM one needed the blue ones to be quiet. Eleven selves, eleven medicine cabinets, and the real her — whoever that was — rattling around underneath like a pill in an empty bottle, making noise but not doing anything.

She'd tried, once, to find out which one was real. Twenty-seven, a bad year, the kind where the city shows you its teeth. She took a week off — told everyone it was exhaustion, which was true the way a map is true — and went through the dolls one by one, wearing each for a full day, taking notes. Monday: boardroom. Felt nothing. Tuesday: camera. Felt watched. By Friday she was down to the last two and she couldn't tell them apart anymore, and that night she took the 3 AM one down off the shelf and just looked at her for a long time. No face. Just weather. A low front moving in.

"What are you for?" she whispered.

And the weather said — not in words, because it didn't have a mouth — I'm the one who's left when the others go home.

That scared her more than anything the industry had ever done to her, and the industry had done plenty. She put the doll back. Took two blue ones. Slept for fourteen hours and woke up as the camera self, because there was a lunch.

The thing about the dolls — the thing nobody tells you when you start collecting — is that they don't stay on the shelf. They leak. She'd be at a dinner as the interesting-man self and catch the boardroom one's posture in the reflection of a wine glass. She'd be doing an interview as the camera self and hear the 3 AM one's weather in her own laugh, low and moving in. They bled into each other at the edges, and the bleeding was the scariest part, because if they could bleed into each other, then the lines between them weren't real, and if the lines weren't real, then there was only one of her, and she was all of them at once, and that was too many people to be at a dinner party.

So she dusted. Every Sunday. Maintenance. You have to maintain yourselves, or they maintain you — and she'd seen what that looked like, on other women, women who'd stopped dusting. They'd show up to set wearing three selves at once, contradicting themselves between takes, and the crew would exchange looks, and somebody would say "she's having a moment," and the moment would last the rest of her life.

Tonight was Sunday. The shelf was clean. The neon was pink through the blinds. And she was still holding the camera doll, turning her slowly, asking the shelf which one gets to leave.

It occurred to her — the way things occur at 3 AM, which is to say it arrived fully formed and undeniable — that she'd been asking the wrong question for years. Not which one leaves. What if none of them are the one who stays? What if the real her wasn't on the shelf at all? What if she was the hand? The hand that dusted, the hand that chose, the hand that held them up to the light. The hand was the only thing that touched all eleven. The hand was the only thing they'd never made a doll of.

She set the camera doll down on the counter. Not back on the shelf — on the counter, facing the wall. A small demotion. A tiny mutiny.

Then she did something she hadn't done in eleven years. She stood in front of the mirror — the big one, the one she practiced in at nineteen — and she didn't put on any of them. No shoulders. No teeth. No weather. Just the hand, and the face behind it, unassigned, undusted, unmaintained. It looked tired. It looked nineteen and thirty-four at the same time. It looked like somebody who'd been managing a staff for a decade and just realized she was the only employee.

"Hi," she said to it.

It didn't answer either. But it didn't need to. For the first time in years, the silence in the bathroom wasn't eleven selves not answering. It was one person, not talking, which is different. Which is almost like listening.

She left the camera doll facing the wall. She'd deal with it in the morning — there was always a morning, there was always a lunch. But tonight, for the first time since she was nineteen and too herself for the gesture, there were only ten dolls on the shelf.

Eleven minus one. It wasn't leaving. Not yet.

But it was a start. And starts, she'd learned from every self she'd ever been, are the only things that count.
