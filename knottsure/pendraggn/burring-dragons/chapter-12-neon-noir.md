# VALLEY OF THE DOLLS: REIMAGINED

## Chapter 12: Neon & Noir

The clinic was underneath the old Schwab's, which was underneath a noodle bar, which was underneath about sixty years of bad decisions. I took the service elevator down, and the neon followed me — pink leaking through the cage bars, striping my coat like a barcode.

"You're late, Welles." The starlet didn't look up from the mirror. She was twenty-four this decade. Great bone structure, rented. The real her was somewhere in the back of her own skull, eighty-nine years old and paying by the hour to stay quiet.

"Traffic on the 101," I said. "It's been congested since '48."

"My grandmother knew your grandmother," she said. "Anne. She was kind to me once, at a party I don't remember." She smiled at the mirror, which is to say she smiled at herself. "That's why I called you instead of one of the chop shops. Family rates for family business."

I set my case on the glass table. "You said 1965."

"I said before the biopic." She turned, and the young face did an old thing with its mouth. "They're making the picture. Some girl from the feeds, twenty years old, cheekbones like switchblades. She'll cry on cue and the audience will forgive everything. But she'll be working from the archive, and the archive has gaps, and gaps make journalists curious." She leaned forward. "There are eleven hours of 1965 I need extracted and burned. Tonight."

Eleven hours is a lot of forgetting. "What happened in 1965?"

"What always happens." She stood and walked to the window, where the neon Schwab's sign buzzed against the rain. "A party. A man. A balcony with a bad railing." She didn't turn around. "I've paid to forget it every ten years since. The procedure gets cheaper and the forgetting gets thinner. This time I want it gone permanently. No backup, no ghost, no deep-storage copy for the estate to find."

I should have walked out. Memory brokers have one rule and it's the same as it ever was: you don't burn evidence. You copy it, you escrow it, you hold it over people. That's the business. But she said Anne — my grandmother's name, which nobody uses except people who knew her — and she said it the way you say a password.

I opened the case.

---

The extraction rig hummed at 50 hertz, the same note as the building's wiring, the same note as thunder. She lay back in the chair and the young face went slack, and then the old one surfaced — just for a second, eighty-nine years of kept secrets looking out through rented eyes.

"Careful with the edges," she whispered. "The good years are load-bearing."

I went in through the temporal gate, the way you go into any stranger's head: politely, like a burglar. 1965 unspooled around me — cigarette smoke and swimming pools, the particular gold of afternoon light on the Valley before the smog got honest. A party. Laughter with teeth in it. A man with a producer's hands, explaining what the balcony was for.

And then the railing, and the sound a body makes when the party keeps going underneath it.

I found the eleven hours. They were wrapped in scar tissue, a decade of forgetting layered over them like bandages. I cut them out clean.

That's when I saw the girl in the corner of the memory.

She was maybe nine. Dark hair, Anne Welles' eyes — my eyes. She was standing in the hallway while the party laughed and the man fell, holding a glass of something orange, watching everything. The starlet's memory had caught her the way a camera catches a stranger in the background of a photograph.

The girl looked straight at me. Straight out of 1965, straight at the broker.

And she said, in my grandmother's voice: "We don't burn family business, sweetheart. We inherit it."

---

I sat up. The rig was still humming. The starlet was asleep in the chair, twenty-four and peaceful, eleven hours lighter.

The extracted memory sat in the buffer, glowing faint amber. Eleven hours. A murder. And a nine-year-old witness with my face, who the starlet had been paying to forget — not because the girl saw the body.

Because the girl was hers. Given up, renamed, raised on stories about a grandmother named Anne who never existed.

I looked at the burn switch. Then I looked at the escrow slot.

The neon buzzed. The rain kept coming. Somewhere upstairs, somebody ordered noodles.

I made a copy.
