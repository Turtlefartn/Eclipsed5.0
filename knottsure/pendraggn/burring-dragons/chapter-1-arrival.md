# VALLEY OF THE DOLLS: REIMAGINED

## Chapter 1: Arrival

The bus let me off on Hollywood Boulevard at 2 AM, which in this city is the same as noon — the neon doesn't care what time it is, it just runs. I had a suitcase, forty dollars, and a face that hadn't learned to be anyone yet. That was the last time I was only one person, though I didn't know it then. Nobody tells you. There's no sign at the city limits that says ABANDON SINGULARITY, ALL YE WHO ENTER. There should be. It would save everybody some time.

My grandmother's letters said the city used to run on pills. Little dolls, she called them, the way you'd call a storm "weather." Red ones to come down, yellow ones to go up, and the white ones that kept you sharp enough to lie for a living. She worked the switchboard at a studio that doesn't exist anymore, and she watched the girls rattle past her desk like pill bottles in an earthquake, and she wrote me that the secret wasn't the drugs. The secret was what the drugs were for.

I should stop here and tell you who I am, because that's what chapter ones do. That's the contract. Fine. My name is Welles. Anne's granddaughter. I came to the city to be in pictures, because that's what girls in my family do instead of learning to swim — we walk into deep water and call it ambition.

Except — hold on. You caught that, right? "That's what chapter ones do." I just said the words chapter one in chapter one. Which means I know I'm in one. Which means —

Let me check something.

[She pauses. Somewhere above her, a pen stops moving.]

Yeah. There's somebody up there, writing this. Writing me pausing. Which means I'm not the one who noticed the contract, am I? I'm the one who was written noticing the contract. That's a worse job. The girl who notices is at least awake. The girl who's written noticing is —

[The pen starts again. She sighs. It sounds like rain on a window she can't see.]

Forget it. Where was I. The bus. Forty dollars. Schwab's.

The old Schwab's is a noodle bar now, but the basement is still the basement, and the basement has a clinic, and the clinic has a waiting room with chairs from four different decades, which is how you know the business is older than the furniture. I wasn't there for the clinic. Not yet. I was there because the woman at the boarding house said the counter girl at Schwab's knew a guy who knew a casting director, and in this city that's practically a blood relation.

The counter girl's name was Lyonne, and she had eight teeth when she smiled, and she looked at my suitcase and my forty dollars and my one unpracticed face, and she said, "Honey, you're early."

"For what?"

"For everything." She poured coffee I hadn't asked for. "The girls who make it here don't arrive. They accumulate. One self for the audition, one for the callback, one for the producer's dinner. You got one face, that's a starter kit."

"I just got here."

"That's what I'm saying. You're early." She leaned in. The neon from the sign bled through the window and striped her face pink, and for a second I saw it — the flicker, the seam, like a mask slipping half a millimeter. "First one's free, by the way. The advice. The rest costs."

I should tell you what I looked like, because the girl writing — no. The girl being written. Me. I should tell you what I looked like because that's also in the contract, appearance first, interiority later, the reader needs a face to hang the trouble on. I had my grandmother's cheekbones and my mother's mouth and eyes that hadn't learned the city yet, which is to say eyes that still believed a face was something you had instead of something you kept.

Here's the thing about the first doll. Nobody remembers making her. You go in for an audition — some room with a folding table and a man who doesn't look up — and he does the thing with his hand, the smoothing thing, the too gesture. Too what, he doesn't say. Too yourself. And you go home, and you stand in front of the boarding-house mirror with its silver coming off at the edges, and you practice being less. Less what. Less you. And it works. You get the callback. And you never once think: I just made something. You think: I just got better.

That's the con at the center of the whole valley, and I'm telling you on page one because the woman upstairs with the pen — the one who stopped and started again, you felt that too, don't lie — she told me to tell you early. She says the middle of the book is where the forgetting happens, so the beginning is where the remembering has to live.

[The pen hovers. In a room that smells like rain, a man asks the woman holding it: "Are you sure you want her to know that?" The woman says: "She's my book. She can know whatever I can afford." The man says: "Can you afford it?" The pen comes down.]

I can't hear them anymore. That's probably for the best.

Lyonne was right. I was early. By the end of the month I had three faces and a prescription I didn't ask for and a Sunday ritual I didn't have a name for yet, and the forty dollars was gone, and the suitcase was under a bed in a room I'd already stopped thinking of as mine, and somewhere in the back of my own skull a girl with one face was paying by the hour to stay quiet.

She's still back there. She writes letters to a grandmother who answers in a hand I recognize as the pen from upstairs, and that's the part I haven't told Lyonne, because Lyonne would charge for the advice and I can't afford it.

The city runs on dolls now. Not the pills — the pills were just the prototype. The dolls are the product. Eleven of them on a shelf, eleven selves, and the real you rattling underneath like a pill in an empty bottle.

I arrived with one. I'm telling you this so that later, when the shelf is full and the dusting has started and the forgetting is load-bearing, you'll remember there was a version of this story where the girl got off the bus and was only one person, and the neon didn't care what time it was, and forty dollars felt like enough.

It wasn't. It never is. That's why they call it the valley.

You go down into it. Everybody does. The only question is how many of you come back up.

~Muse
