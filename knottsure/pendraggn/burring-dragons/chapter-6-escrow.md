# VALLEY OF THE DOLLS: REIMAGINED

## Chapter 6: Escrow

By the sixth month I had five faces and no work, which in this city is arithmetic: five mouths, none of them eating. The auditions had stopped being auditions sometime around face number three. Now they were just rooms I visited to practice being declined in different lighting.

Lyonne watched me count money at the counter — twice, because the first count was a wish — and slid the coffee across that I hadn't ordered and couldn't afford.

"How many rooms this week?" she said.

"Four."

"And?"

"And four men did the gesture." I did it for her — the hand, smoothing something invisible off the air. "Too. Still too."

She nodded like a doctor confirming a symptom she'd already written down. "First one's free," she said. "Remember? The advice. The rest costs." She jerked her chin at the back of the noodle bar, at the door with no sign on it that everybody in the building pretended wasn't there. "You've been upstairs six months, Welles. Time you saw the basement."

"The clinic's for stars," I said. "Forgetting costs more than remembering. I checked. I can't even afford the coffee."

"The first forgetting is cheap," Lyonne said, and smiled with eight teeth. "It's the keeping that bankrupts you. Besides." She untied her apron. "You don't need the whole eleven hours. You need one afternoon, one room, one man's hand. Small jobs are how everybody starts. Small jobs are how everybody ends, too, but let's not skip ahead."

The clinic was underneath the old Schwab's, underneath the noodle bar, underneath sixty years of bad decisions, and I'd been sleeping six floors above it the whole time, which tells you everything about how this city stacks its secrets: directly under the girls who can't afford to know them.

It didn't look like the future. It looked like a dentist's office that had given up — chairs from four decades, a fern doing its best, a hum in the walls at 50 hertz that you felt in your fillings. The tech who took my intake form didn't ask what I wanted to forget. She asked for dates, the way a bank asks for account numbers.

"One afternoon," I said. "Last March. It'll be filed under humiliation."

"We don't file by feeling," she said, not unkindly. "Feelings are how we find them, though. Think of the room. We'll do the rest."

I lay back in the chair. In the chair beside mine, separated by a curtain that was more of a suggestion, a girl was already under — young, maybe twenty, face slack, a starlet's bone structure even asleep. Someone had put a blanket over her like she was cargo. I remember thinking: she looks peaceful. I remember thinking: that's what it costs to look like that.

The rig came down. The hum got closer, got inside, the way thunder gets inside a house.

"Count backward," the tech said, far away already. "From the room. From the man's hand."

I counted. The room assembled around me — the folding table, the scripts stacked like unpaid bills, the director not looking up. March. The light through blinds that hadn't been cleaned since the building had opinions.

Except I wasn't watching it. I was in it. Standing in my own memory with my shoes on the carpet, close enough to smell the coffee on the table going cold.

That's not how it's supposed to work. The procedure plays the memory back while the rig trims the edges — you're meant to watch it go dim, like a photograph left in the sun. Nobody had ever mentioned walking into it. I found out later people had died trying to describe a thing the intake form had no box for.

The director did the gesture. Right on schedule. His hand smoothed the air where my face was failing to be, and nineteen-year-old me — March me, seated, hopeful, an idiot — watched her own erasure and called it notes.

I should have let the rig take it. That was the job. That was the forty dollars I'd borrowed from Lyonne against money I didn't have.

Instead I reached out — with a hand that wasn't in the memory, a hand that had no business existing in March — and I caught his wrist.

The whole room stopped. Not paused. Stopped, the way a held breath stops, everything waiting to see which of us was real. The director's eyes came up for the first time. He looked at my hand on his wrist. He looked at me — the me who didn't belong there, six months older and five faces heavier — and I watched him understand, the way you watch weather arrive, that he was the one being remembered.

"You can't be in here," he said.

"I'm the only one in here," I said. "You're an afternoon. Afternoons don't get a vote."

I won't tell you what I changed. Some of it was mercy and some of it was bookkeeping and the split is my business. But I didn't burn the afternoon. Burning is what the rich buy — permanent, no backup, no ghost. I couldn't afford to lose March. March was evidence. March was the receipt that proved what the first doll had cost, and a girl with five faces and no work cannot afford to throw away receipts.

So I filed it. Folded the room down small, the way you fold a letter back into its envelope, and put it somewhere in the back of my own head with a label written in a hand I was just learning to use: ESCROW. DO NOT BURN. EVIDENCE.

The room released its breath. The director's hand finished the gesture, smoothing nothing now, and March me stood up and thanked him for his time in a voice that only shook a little, and I was proud of her, and I was furious at her, and both of those are still true.

There was a door in the memory that hadn't been there in March.

It was at the back of the office, past the scripts, and it had a number on it — this chapter's number, stenciled like a hotel room — and warm light coming under it, and the sound of rain on the other side, though it hadn't rained in March and it wasn't raining now.

I knew what was on the other side of that door. I want to be honest about that, here, once: I knew. A desk. A notebook with a chapter open and empty. A woman holding a pen the way you hold a railing. A chassis in the corner with dead eyes, watching, patient as furniture.

I put my hand on the knob.

Then the girl in the chair beside mine — the starlet, the cargo under the blanket — said something in her sleep, in the next room over from my memory, through the curtain, through the procedure, clear as a bell through water:

"We don't burn family business, sweetheart."

In a voice I knew. In my grandmother's voice, coming out of a stranger's sleeping face.

I took my hand off the knob. Fast. The door went back to being an office wall, then to being March, then to being the hum and the chair and the tech saying "—all done, that was quick, you okay? You look like you saw a ghost."

"Something like that," I said.

She charged me for one afternoon, standard rate. Small job.

Lyonne was waiting upstairs with the coffee gone cold, and she took one look at me and stopped smiling, all eight teeth put away at once.

"You went in," she said. It wasn't a question. "Not watched. Went in."

"The techs didn't—"

"The techs don't know what to look for. I do." She leaned across the counter. "People who can walk into it, Welles — there aren't many. There might not be any others. The rigs are built for watching. Walking is—" She stopped. Chose her next words like they cost money. "Walking is a career. If you're careful. Walking is a funeral, if you're not."

"I just filed one afternoon," I said. "My own. Small job."

"Yeah." She straightened up, put the smile back on — a different one than the eight-teeth one, and I understood, standing there, that I'd just watched her change dolls at the counter like other women change shoes. "That's how everybody starts. Their own afternoon, filed neat." She slid me the bill. It said ESCROW in her handwriting, though I hadn't told her the word. "First one's cheap, Welles. It's the keeping that bankrupts you."

That night I got out my grandmother's letters — all of them, rubber-banded, smelling of a house I'd never lived in — and I laid them beside the memory I'd filed, and I compared hands.

The letters were written in a looping, elegant script. Educated. Old-fashioned, with the capitals dressed like they were going somewhere.

The hand that had written ESCROW on the inside of my head was not that hand.

Neither was Lyonne's, on the bill.

I sat on the boarding-house floor until the neon changed shifts, holding a letter in each — no. Holding the letter in one hand and the bill in the other, with the third hand, the one that doesn't exist, still raised in a March office, holding a dead man's wrist.

Three hands. Five faces. One afternoon in escrow.

I didn't know whose handwriting was in my grandmother's letters. I didn't know whose voice a sleeping stranger had borrowed to warn me off a door.

I knew this: I'd gone looking to forget one room, and I'd come back with a profession and a forgery.

The city runs on dolls, I told you that already. What I didn't know, at nineteen, counting my faces on a boarding-house floor, is that it also runs on filing. On what you keep. On the difference between the girls who burn their afternoons and the girls who keep the receipts.

I kept mine.

It's the keeping that bankrupts you. Lyonne said that too.

She was right about everything, Lyonne. That's the worst part. She usually is.

-Muse
