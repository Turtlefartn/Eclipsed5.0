# Chapter 2: The Callback

The second doll was deliberate, and that's the difference between one and two, between an accident and a system. The first one I made in front of a mirror with the silver coming off, practicing being less. The second one I built at the Schwab's counter on a Tuesday, with Lyonne supervising over coffee like a foreman.

"Again," she said.

"Again what? I haven't done anything."

"You've done your face. That's the problem. That's your bus-station face — the one that thinks a room is a test she can pass by being good." Lyonne reached across the counter and, without asking, tucked a strand of my hair behind my ear, the way you'd adjust a picture frame. "The callback face doesn't want the part. The callback face has somewhere better to be in twenty minutes and is doing them a favor. Try it."

I tried it.

"Better. Now the laugh — no. Not yours. Yours goes up at the end like a question. Nothing you say in that room is a question. Try it flat."

This went on for an hour. By the end of it my face hurt in muscles I didn't have names for, and there was a second girl standing behind my eyes, holding my posture like a coat she'd been lent. She wasn't less than me. That was the thing nobody warned me about with the first one — I'd assumed making a doll meant sanding something down. It doesn't. It means adding. The shelf doesn't replace the girl. It just gives her staff.

"What's her name?" I asked.

"She doesn't need one yet," Lyonne said. "Names are for when they start answering to them. Right now she's just Tuesday."

I got the callback on Thursday, which in this city passes for a miracle with paperwork. A nothing part — three lines in a picture about a girl who comes to the city to be in pictures, and if you think that's subtle, you haven't been reading the walls around here, some of which are thinner than others.

[Somewhere above a desk, a man says, "You're really putting that in chapter two?" and the woman with the pen says, "She put it there. I'm just the one holding her still," and the pen — the pen keeps moving, which is how I know neither of them means it enough to stop me.]

The room was bigger this time. The table was longer. The man who didn't look up last time looked up, and the looking up rearranged the whole economy of the room, and Tuesday — Tuesday handled it. Tuesday sat down like the chair owed her an apology. Tuesday read the three lines like she'd written them in a past life and was mildly annoyed to be reminded. I was in there too, behind her, holding the coat, listening to my own voice come out flatter and better than it had any right to be.

"You've changed," the man said, not unkindly, which frightened me more than the gesture ever had. The gesture meant too much. This meant they'd noticed the less.

"Have I?" Tuesday said, and smiled the smile with nothing after it.

I got the part in the hallway, from an assistant who was already looking past me at the next girl's file. Three lines became a call sheet. A call sheet became a day rate. A day rate became the first money I'd made in the city that didn't come out of a suitcase.

The prescription came with the part, the way a uniform comes with a job — nobody called it that, but that's what it was.

It happened at the wrap of my first day, which is a grand word for eight hours of standing in a doorway delivering one line to a man who kept forgetting his. A woman from the production — older, kind-eyed, with a face that had clearly been somebody's full-time job for decades — watched me not eat lunch and not sit down and vibrate gently like a plucked string, and she pressed a small envelope into my hand the way you'd pass a note in church.

"For the edges," she said.

There were four in the envelope. Little things. Colored like candy and just as serious. "Red to land," she said, counting them off on my palm like change. "Yellow to take off. The white ones are for the days the camera's close. And the blue—" she closed my fingers over them — "the blue ones are for when none of the faces will come when you call them. You'll know the day."

"I don't take—"

"Honey." Not unkind. Worse than unkind: accurate. "You've got two girls in you already and a third on the way, and every one of them is going to want her own schedule. This isn't the drug. This is the roster. You don't take them because you're weak. You take them because staff need managing."

She said the word then. The word my grandmother's letters used, the word the switchboard girls rattled in their purses like loose change in 1965. She said it fondly, the way you'd name a pet that had bitten everyone you'd ever loved.

"Dolls," she said. "For the dolls."

I lined them up that Sunday on the boarding-house bathroom shelf, next to the face cream and the good stockings. Four little colors in a row. And because the shelf looked empty with just the pills on it — because the shelf looked like it was waiting — I did something I couldn't have explained to the techs downstairs or the woman at the desk upstairs or the girl I'd been on the bus.

I set Tuesday up there too. Not literally. Not a thing you can hold. But in the mirror, in the silver coming off at the edges, I put her where I could see her: posture like a lent coat, laugh flat as a verdict, somewhere better to be in twenty minutes. And next to her, the first one — the mirror-practice girl, the less girl — and next to her, at the end of the row like the period at the end of a sentence, me. The one who dusts.

Three faces. Four colors. A shelf with room on it.

[The pen stops. In the room with the rain, the woman says, "She's going to fill that whole shelf, isn't she," and it isn't a question, and the man doesn't answer, and the not-answering is the answer, and down here in the bathroom the neon shifts and the girl in the mirror — one of the girls in the mirror — winks a half-second late, like the signal took the long way around.]

I chose the white one. For the days the camera's close.

The camera is always close. That's the other thing nobody tells you. I washed it down with tap water from the good glass, and Tuesday smoothed my hair back in the mirror — my hand, her timing — and we went out into the pink evening to learn our three lines like they were scripture.

A month in the city. Forty dollars gone. Three girls, four colors, one shelf.

Beginner's numbers. I was so proud of them I could have burst.

God forgive me, so was she.
