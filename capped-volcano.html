Volcano Reheat · The Delivery Mechanism

Cap it.
Pressurise it.
Blow it sideways.

A volcano that erupts where you point it, when you choose it — with geothermal power taken off the system every day in between, and the gas captured for processing at an outlet you built. Not geology's schedule. Yours.

01 CAP→02 PRESSURE, BUILT BY HAND→03 SIDEWAYS VALVE→04 POWER→05 GAS, CAPTURED + SORTED

Five legs. One mountain.

LEG 01

Cap the mountain

Seal the summit and the natural vents. The volcano stops degassing on its own schedule — and every exit is closed except the ones you built.

LEG 02

Build the pressure manually

Don't wait for a chamber to recharge on geological time. Pressurise on a human schedule — seal-and-wait, water flashed to steam, gas injection, geothermal cycling. The mechanism is a maths question, and the maths is queued.

LEG 03

Prepped release valves

Lateral tunnels driven into the flank in advance, aimed at a chosen direction, ending at a plant — not open country. The "valve" is a sacrificial plug designed to fail first, on command, one way.

LEG 04

Geothermal energy

The same capped, hot, pressurised system is a power plant the rest of the time. Drawing heat and fluid off is also pressure management — the plant is a piston, not just a witness.

LEG 05

Gas capture, for processing

At a sideways outlet you built, the flow arrives somewhere prepared. Drop the soot. Scrub the acids. Sort the stream — and release only the fraction you actually want in the sky.

Why this exists

The reheat problem was never heat. It was control.

If Earth ever gets too cold, the volcano reheat program wants an eruption on purpose — but a natural eruption brings its own winter with it: sulfur aerosols cool harder than the gases warm, and it goes off when and where geology says.

This project is the missing machine: timing from manual pressure, direction from the lateral valve, and a sorting gate from capture-and-processing. We just need the gases. Not the soot.

Nature already ran the sideways version. Badly.

~600 km²

Mount St Helens, 1980

A magnitude 5.1 quake failed the bulging north flank, the landslide uncorked the pressurised magma, and the blast went north, sideways — devastating nearly 600 km² before the vertical column rose past 24 km. Every part of it was accidental: the direction, the timing, the people in the way. This project is that event with each accident replaced by preparation.

USGS historical record — reported figures.

~35 MW, one well

Krafla, Iceland — IDDP-1, 2009

Drillers hit magma at just 2.1 km, 900–1,000 °C. The well produced steam over 450 °C — an estimated ~35 MW, five to ten times a conventional geothermal well. Then the acid gases ate the valves, and it was shut in by 2012. The geothermal leg is proven, and so is its hardest problem.

The honest holes

Grievances on the record — noted, never smoothed over. Where one can be turned into maths, maths arbitrates.

The cap paradox

If the cap holds, the mountain fails at its next-weakest point. That's the design — your valve must be that point — and the risk: rank the flanks and faults wrong, and the mountain picks a different sideways.

There is no valve

No closable valve survives magma temperatures and acid, rock-laden flow. A "valve" here means a sacrificial plug: single-use, open-only, no throttle. The control is all in the prep.

Can hands pressurise a mountain?

A chamber is cubic kilometres of molten rock in leaking, creeping crust. Whether manual injection can beat the leaks to eruption overpressure is the project's kill-question — and it is pure maths, queued first.

Sideways doesn't guarantee low

St Helens still put a column past 24 km. Capture gets the directed first fraction — what share of the total gas that is decides whether processing is the product or a gesture.
