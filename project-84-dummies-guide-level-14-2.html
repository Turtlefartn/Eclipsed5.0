Level 14 · School Edition Project 84 for Dummies — the fleet guide

Project 84 is a fleet.
Here is the fleet.

Project 84 is a network of floating anchors, the HEAR-TS work boats that run between them, the support vessels that keep those boats running — and the people using all of it, for whatever purpose the hardware is kitted out for. This guide covers the systems: the numbers, the specs, the routes, the site rules, the jobs, and the crews and passengers who do them.

00

Who is in Project 84? Anyone using the kit

Project 84 is not just the metal. It is the hardware and the people using that hardware — and what those people are doing is set by how the hardware is kitted out, not by a fixed mission list. The same HEAR-TS hull can be kitted as a passenger shuttle, a waste boat, a Fish Bath station, or an orca-support craft. Whoever crews it, rides it, or works from it is part of Project 84 — doing whatever job that kit is for.

  • At the anchorsThe residents who live there and the workers who run them — power, hydrogen, docks, and daily life.
  • On the HEAR-TSThe crews who sail them, and the passengers they carry anchor to anchor and anchor to shore.
  • On the support vesselsThe well-dock crews and the rotating maintenance teams who keep the fleet repaired, fuelled, and relaunched.
  • On each configured jobShuttle crews, waste handlers, Fish Bath operators, lane-light tenders, orca-support trainers, and the hurricane-research teams — the user follows the kit.

The rule is simple: you belong to Project 84 by using the hardware as kitted, whatever that kit's purpose is. Re-kit the same boat and the same person's job in 84 changes with it. What 84 does not include is a government: chairs, councils, and succession belong to a different document (the Proclamation of Claim) and stay out of this guide.

01

The anchors — home bases at sea

An anchor is a home base at sea: a permanent floating settlement that a region of the fleet works out of. People live there, boats dock there, hydrogen is made there. Everything else in Project 84 either sits at an anchor or travels between them.

Dozens of anchors in the network — a floor, not a final count
Tens of thousands of people at each anchor, at minimum
Millions of people across the whole network, at minimum

Where can an anchor go? The site rules

Anchor sites have to pass a checklist before they are even candidates:

  • Well over a hundred kilometres from the nearest shore — open ocean, not coastal water.
  • Calm water, or productive currents — sheltered seas, or currents that carry nutrients and life.
  • Low cyclone exposure — outside the regular hurricane and typhoon tracks.
  • Year-round sun — for power and for hydrogen production.
  • Spread across every ocean — Pacific, Atlantic, Indian, Southern, and Arctic — so the network is global, not regional.

About the candidate-site study list

There is a separate, first-pass study list of dozens of candidate sites — mostly in the Pacific and Atlantic, with Indian, Southern, and Arctic sites too — drawn up to be poked at and argued with. It is a study list, not the anchor list. No source says the anchors were picked from those sites — the study list and the anchor count come from different documents, and this guide keeps them separate.

02

HEAR-TS — the work boats

HEAR-TS craft are the capillaries of the system: short-hop boats running anchor-to-anchor and anchor-to-shore. They are not deep-ocean cruisers — they work coastal and inter-site legs, carrying people, freight, and everything the anchors can't make for themselves. They run on hydrogen fuel cells, tied into the hydrogen produced at the settlements.

The jobs

  • Shuttle runsPeople and freight between anchors, and from each anchor to its nearest shore port.
  • Waste collectionHEAR-TS collect sewage from settlements and capsules, treat it onboard, and discharge treated effluent as nutrients. Waste becomes feedstock, not pollution.
  • Fish BathsModular UV cleaning stations deployed from HEAR-TS hulls — dockside, at aquaculture pens, or at capsule clusters. Fish pass through a chemical-free UV bath that knocks down parasites and pathogens.
  • Lane lightsThe lit lanes marking the routes between sites. HEAR-TS print lane-light components onboard and service the lanes they sail.
  • Orca supportA dedicated assignment — so big it gets its own section below (Section 5).
  • Hurricane workAn early research track, not a proven job: whether a fleet can weaken storms at sea. Listed honestly as research — the physics is brutal and nothing is field-proven.
03

How many people? It depends which craft you mean

This is where people trip up. The living pod and the trimaran are different designs from different files, so their “how many people” sizes are different calculations — not three estimates of the same thing, and never to be averaged, merged, or swapped for each other.

Capacity sizes don't merge

A habitat pod houses a handful of people for normal living, and its emergency shelter maths — usable volume at a fixed allowance per person — works out to dozens of people. Both figures come from pill-habitats.md. The HEAR-TS trimaran carries hundreds of passengers per trip — from hear-ts.md. A handful, dozens, and hundreds are three answers to three different questions. Never quote one as another's spec.

04

Support vessels — the well-dock motherships

Small boats out on the routes can't wait on shore logistics. So the fleet carries its own garages: well-dock motherships — huge ships with a floodable well deck in the stern. A HEAR-TS sails in, the well drains or the ship ballasts down, and the boat is lifted, repaired, refuelled, and relaunched without ever touching shore. Think floating maintenance bay, not warship: the design deliberately strips out troop decks, weapons, and combat systems, and keeps the workshop.

Planned fleetThousands of ships

Boats per shipAbout a dozen HEAR-TS — a few in the well, more parked on deck and hangar
CrewA hundred or so, plus rotating maintenance teams
Kept aboardMaintenance workshops, onboard 3D printers, hydrogen bunkering and storage, spares storage, heavy craneage, a clinic, and a VTOL pad for moving people (crew rotation, technicians, trainers).
Thrown overboardTroop berthing at assault scale, full aviation operations, all weapons, combat radars and sensors, tank decks, assault-force medical.

Multiply it out and the mothership fleet berths a huge share of the fleet's boats at any one time. That garage capacity is as much a part of Project 84 as the boats themselves — these ships are very expensive to build, and shipyard time, not money alone, is the binding constraint on how fast the support fleet can grow.

Maintenance docks are the second support type: open-air drydocks for HEAR-TS upkeep, designed as twin-hull SWATH vessels (a stable, wide work platform). Still open: one dock per anchor versus shared yards, and shore-based versus floating.

05

Orca support — the first boats have a job

The very first production run has a job before it has routes. The first HEAR-TS built are assigned to orca support — a pair of boats for every wild orca, across a wild population of tens of thousands of orcas. One boat is active; the other is the spare, rotating in for resupply so coverage never gaps.

First production run goes to orca support hear-ts.md
A pair of boats for every wild orca — one active, one spare on rotation hear-ts.md
Tens of thousands of wild orcas covered

What an orca-support boat actually does: transport for the pod's people, communications relay (including the keyboard uplink — the two-way link the cetacean communication work runs on), fish resupply, a trainer berth, and a med kit. Everything the pod needs, at the pod's pace.

That pace is the catch. Orcas burst far faster than the standard trimaran cruises — that burst speed is the figure hear-ts.md designs against — and the standard hull can't be the thing slowing the pod down. So the orca boats need to be a fast variant — probably smaller and leaner than the standard freighter hull. Its spec is still to be decided; that's an open hole, not a solved design. One part of the loadout is settled, though: every orca-support HEAR-TS carries a crate of beach balls — the big ones. Non-negotiable.
