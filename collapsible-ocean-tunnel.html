Side Quest SQ-12 · Ocean Transport

Vacuum in front.
Wave behind.
Let the ocean shove.

A hyperloop-esque tunnel in the ocean, about 20 metres down — except the tunnel is collapsible, the train rides in the water inside it, and nothing on board has to push. The tunnel opens ahead of the train and the opening makes the vacuum. Behind it, the tunnel collapses again — and the ocean does the rest.

01 COLLAPSED, IDLE→02 OPENS AHEAD→03 VACUUM IN FRONT→04 TRAIN RIDES THE WATER→05 COLLAPSE BEHIND PUSHES→06 RECOLLAPSE · REPEAT

Just two ingredients.

Not an engine on the train. Not a pump farm evacuating an ocean of pipe. Two things, and the sea itself:

INGREDIENT 01

The vacuum in front

The tunnel ahead is collapsed — near-zero volume, sealed. As the train approaches, that section opens. A volume created from nothing, with nothing rushing in to fill it, is a vacuum. The train's nose meets no air, no water column, no bow wave. Nothing to push out of the way.

INGREDIENT 02

The collapse behind

Behind the train, the tunnel recollapses — the wave. Water refills and is expelled as the volume behind the train is driven toward zero, so the train cannot fall back and the pressure has exactly one way to go: forward, into the train.

THE ENGINE

Ocean pressure provides the thrust

At depth, the sea is already squeezing everything, all the time, for free. A rigid tunnel spends its life fighting that squeeze. This one spends it: the differential across the train — vacuum at the nose, depth pressure at the tail — is the motor.

Why not a normal hyperloop?

A hyperloop's hardest problems are the ones this deletes.

A conventional hyperloop is a rigid tube holding near-vacuum against full outside pressure along every metre, forever — in the ocean, that means fighting ~3 atmospheres of crush with structure, seals and pumps, full-time, everywhere. Here the tube is collapsed when nobody's using it: no vacuum to maintain, no crush load to resist, nothing inside to flood. The vacuum exists only where a train is about to be, only for as long as it takes to pass — and the wall makes it by opening, not a pump by sucking. When the train has gone, the tunnel floods, collapses, and waits.

First maths — labelled for what it is

~20 m

The depth — the project's own figure

Deep enough to sit under the weather and the worst of the wave action, shallow enough to build, inspect and repair. It is also the thrust budget: depth is what the thrust is made of, so the depth and the push are the same decision.

Set figure for the project.

~3 atmospheres

The pressure at that depth

About 3.02 bar absolute (2.01 bar over surface pressure). On an example 3-metre train face, vacuum ahead vs. depth pressure behind gives up to roughly 2,138 kN of push — far more than a train wants, which means the system throttles by wave and seal, not by lack of force.

First-pass calculation. The 3 m size is an example only — no diameter is set.

Status

Much science needed.

That is the honest label, and it goes on the front, not in the footnotes. This page is a concept at the question stage — opened 25 October 2026 and set for research. The unknowns below are the research, in roughly the order they bite:

Materials — no idea yet. Literally. What folds flat a million times, seals while folded, opens true, shrugs off seawater and fouling, does not exist on a shelf. Requirements first, substances second.
The energy loop. Opening ahead costs about what recollapse behind returns. The concept only closes if the two are coupled so the ocean's work recirculates and you pay only the losses.
Guidance. Rails do not collapse. Whatever guides the train must fold with the wall and re-form exactly, every time.
The seal. The train is a moving bulkhead between vacuum and pressure water. History's warning is on the record: Brunel's atmospheric railway worked, and died on its seal.

The honest holes

Kept on the page, on purpose. A hole you can name is a research item; a hole you hide is a surprise.

Water meets vacuum — where, exactly?

The train rides in water; ahead of it is vacuum. The shape of that boundary, segment by segment — how the pocket forms in a water-carrying tube, how long it holds, how the train crosses and reseals — is research item number one.

Folding is a fatigue machine

Every passage folds and unfolds every metre of wall. Sharp creases crack; the fold has to roll, not crease — a geometry problem wearing a materials costume.

Stopping, and getting stuck

Braking presumably reverses the trick — pressure ahead, release behind — unbuilt. And a train stalled mid-line sits in the only open pocket for miles, with collapsed tube behind it. Rescue means re-opening the line to reach it.

Getting on and off

Boarding means crossing from a surface-pressure station into a system whose front is vacuum and whose back is water at depth pressure. A lock section that cycles states sets the timetable as much as the trains do.

Where it sits

Side Quest SQ-12, filed next to the ocean work.

If it ever leaves research, this is the transport layer between the ocean settlements of the D-Mom ocean track and the anchors of Project 84 — and a sibling of the Arctic idea of walling a shipping lane and running a train through it. There, nature builds the walls. Here, the ocean supplies the pressure. Same move, twice: let the environment do the heavy work.
