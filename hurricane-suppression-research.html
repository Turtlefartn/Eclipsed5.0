Early-stage feasibility / 09 Oct 2026

Can we steer a hurricane?

Two jobs, split cleanly: steer with a smaller asymmetric intervention; keep HEAR-TS on dissipation through cooling, misting, mixing, cloud brightening, bubble curtains, and ocean blankets.

Steering could cut the energy demand. Precision becomes the wall.

A smaller asymmetric cooled patch is the hypothesis; no model yet sizes it or proves a controllable deflection. Any tens-of-kilometres nudge would sit inside today’s 100–200 km three-day forecast error. HEAR-TS keep the dissipation job.

01 / Fuel line

A hurricane is an ocean-fed heat engine.

Warm surface water drives evaporation; condensation releases that stored heat ahead of, or next to, the storm. Ocean heat content through depth matters more than a warm skin alone.

Warm oceanRoughly 26.5°C or warmer through about 50–60 m supports formation and intensification.
→
EvaporationLatent heat is roughly 90% of air–sea enthalpy flux in high winds; spray and foam amplify it.
→
CondensationMoisture rises, condenses, and releases heat that powers eyewall and rainband convection.

Threshold and depth are canonical rules of thumb; ocean heat content in the upper ~50–100 m is the stronger intensity predictor. [1]

Average hurricane heat release600 TW5.2×10¹⁹ J/day through condensation — about 200× world electrical generating capacity. [2]
Power maintaining winds~1.5 TWAbout 1/400 of the heat-release rate; the rest drives thunderstorm updrafts. [2]
Ideal cooled patch260,000 km²720 × 360 km, cooled 2°C through ~80 m in the key model. [3]
Best central-pressure effect≤15%At most ~14 hPa weakening at landfall, despite perfect placement and instantaneous cooling. [3]
02 / Keep it cool

Stop chasing storms. Hold the water near 26.5°C all season.

This is the strategically coherent version of ocean cooling: precondition tropical genesis corridors before a cyclone exists, then maintain them. It trades an impossible three-day sprint for a permanent regional climate-control obligation.

Planning threshold≤26.5°C79.7°F at the surface, with the upper ocean cooled too

A target, not an off switch.

The familiar ~26.5°C threshold is a rule of thumb for formation and intensification, not a binary cutoff. Marginal storms can still form, ocean heat content through roughly the upper 50–100 m matters, and an existing storm weakens gradually rather than switching off the instant it crosses cooler water. [1]

That means “at or near” is not enough by itself: the controlled region needs depth, width, and persistence, plus margin for currents, eddies, forecast error, and warm water around it.

Credit: Salter and Myhrvold

This is what the Salter Sink concept was actually built around: wave-powered devices left in hurricane genesis regions year-round, not boats racing a formed storm. Myhrvold’s public estimate was about 10,000 passive devices at a few thousand dollars each. The effect remains unproven in the field. [6]

Best architecture in this set for sustained preconditioning.

The maintenance-energy trap

Hlywiak’s reported 4.5×10¹⁹ kJ was a one-time cooling estimate for 260,000 km². A standing system must fight continual solar input—roughly 1 kW/m² at peak in this Fermi framing—and warm currents carrying heat back in. Holding 5–10 million km² means maintaining roughly 20–40 times the patch area; on midpoint assumptions, that is about an order of magnitude beyond a 6.5 TW fleet. [3]

The standing battle is larger than the one-time patch.

Full regional climate engineering

Holding tropical water roughly 2°C cooler all season would shift evaporation, convection, rainfall, monsoons, nutrient cycles, and marine ecosystems. If a large seasonal system stopped abruptly, the suppressed heat input would return while storms were still possible—the same termination-shock class of risk raised for marine cloud brightening. [5] [13]

Control note: a planned 2°C change would be phased at no more than 0.1°C per year over 20 years, with each step treated as a testable variable and paused if effects depart from the model. The aim is not to erase every storm. Weaker storms can still happen; keep them below Category 4, aiming for Category 3 or lower.

Verdict: this is the only ocean-cooling strategy its own proponents designed around the right timescale. It also converts a per-storm engineering problem into permanent planetary life support.

03 / Option set

Eight ways to attack the problem. None is field-ready.

Filter by the only plausible use case: seasonal preconditioning, a field research candidate, or a present non-starter.

Bubble curtains

Field candidate

Compressed air released at 100–150 m entrains colder deep water upward.

Evidence
SINTEF model only; no Gulf field demonstration found.
Scale
A 30 km curtain modeled a 30 × 90 km cooled patch after 48 hours, with ~2.8°C surface cooling. [4]
Hull job
Compressor, deep perforated hose, station-keeping.

Bulk seawater misting

Test the mechanism

Atomize seawater above the surface to cool droplets and near-surface air; use deep water if the aim is to import cold rather than recycle warm surface water.

Evidence
Three patents found; no hurricane-relevant field result. One abandoned application’s headline cooling test measured a weather station over sprayed ground, not ocean heat content. [15]
Scale
Another abandoned application proposed 10,000 wave pumps across 25,900 km², one per square mile. [16]
Hull job
Deep-water hose, pump, atomizing nozzles; distinct from cloud-brightening microdroplets.

Airship sheet windbreaks

Force-limited

Hang broad sheets at several altitudes to add drag, extract wind energy, and manufacture local shear.

Evidence
Distributed drag is physically credible; a 2014 model found very large offshore wind farms could reduce hurricane winds and surge. No airship-sheet study was found. [18]
Scale
A 1 km² sheet in 60 m/s wind takes ~2.2 GN, about 225,000 tonnes-force — order-of-magnitude Fermi arithmetic.
Platform
Airships are better used ahead of the storm for spray or sensing; they cannot station-keep in eyewall winds.

Marine cloud brightening

Precondition

Spray fine sea salt into marine clouds so they reflect more sunlight and cool the sea surface over time.

Evidence
Published calculations; no outdoor hurricane-scale test.
Scale
Original concept: ~1,500 vessels, each spraying ~50 m³/s. [5]
Hull job
Atomizers and Flettner rotors; best deck-level fit.

Salter Sinks

Precondition

Wave-powered floating rings push warm surface water down a ~200 m tube for mixing.

Evidence
No field proof; later progress not found.
Scale
Myhrvold estimated ~10,000 passive devices at a few thousand dollars apiece. [6]
Hull job
Tender and maintenance platform, not the primary pump.

Powered upwelling

Scale test clears

Pumps or propellers mix cold deep water into the warm upper layer.

Evidence
Patents and papers; no hurricane-scale validation.
Scale
At 12.9 million hulls × a generous 0.5 MW, the fleet clears the 0.3–3 TW single-patch Fermi band. Worse-than-assumed mixing efficiency can still push demand past it.
Hull job
100–200 m riser and pump; severe pre-storm handling problem.

Submarine churn

Wrong platform

Use submerged propulsive wakes to create a “virtual early landfall” of mixed, cooler water.

Evidence
2020 paper only; no experiment.
Scale
Claimed ~10 nuclear submarines for a 30 × 120 mile patch, with a theoretical COP near 10⁴. [7]
Hull job
Not a surface-workboat method.

Stormfury seeding

Failed baseline

Silver iodide was meant to force eyewall replacement by freezing supercooled water.

Evidence
US trials, 1962–1983.
Failure
Too little supercooled water; apparent effects matched natural eyewall cycles. NOAA concluded it was neither microphysically nor statistically feasible. [8]
04 / Misting water

The nozzle works. The ocean-scale claim does not.

The three bulk-mist proposals split cleanly: warm-surface mist recycles already-warm water, while the other two import cold water from depth. Marine cloud brightening is a separate aerosol method that targets reflectivity on a much slower timescale.

Warm surface mist

Fast local cooling, wrong proof

An abandoned 2020 application claimed a 25.6°C-to-15°C drop after about 20 minutes. Its described experiment sprayed the air above a ground-based weather station; it did not cool or measure an ocean water column. Atomizing warm surface water also adds vapor directly to the hurricane’s fuel-rich boundary layer. [15]

Cold deep-water mist

Upwelling through a nozzle

A 2011 abandoned application proposed wave-powered pumps drawing cold water from depth and spraying it above the sea: 10,000 units, spaced about one per square mile over 25,900 km². That is a patent proposal, not a measured result, and it inherits the same deep-hose, pumping, forecast, and ecological constraints as powered upwelling. [16]

Inside-storm mist

Highest risk, weakest evidence

A granted patent proposes floating wind-turbine pumps that use storm winds to spray deep cold water into the air. It claims faster heat transfer and wind braking from the droplets, but supplies no hurricane field validation. Operating tall spray machinery inside the storm adds the hardest survival and station-keeping case. [17]

Research verdictLocal cooling ≠ storm suppressionwarm-water mist moves heat and moisture into the air instead of removing it from the coupled system

Best experiment: separate the mechanisms.

Test warm surface spray, cold deep-water spray, and unsprayed controls over a bounded patch. Measure upper-ocean heat content, air temperature, humidity, droplet drift, and net enthalpy flux—not just the temperature beside a nozzle.

Fleet fit: spray gear fits a 50 m hull, but bulk misting is still a pump-and-area problem. Warm-water mist may locally cool the surface, but it does not remove energy from the coupled ocean–atmosphere system and may increase storm-fueling humidity. Cold-water mist does not erase Hlywiak’s 260,000 km² target.

Do not conflate it with marine cloud brightening: that method uses very fine sea-salt aerosol to change cloud reflectivity over weeks. Bulk water mist is an immediate heat-and-moisture intervention.

05 / Airship windbreaks

Drag is real. The hanging sheet is not survivable.

Airships could suspend repurposed blanket material at several heights to oppose the flow. The intended mechanism—distributed drag and manufactured shear—is legitimate. The sheet loads make this implementation collapse before it reaches useful scale.

One 1 km² sheet at 60 m/s~2.2 GN≈225,000 tonnes-force across the sheet

The atmosphere turns the sheet into a giant sail.

Using dynamic pressure q = ½ρv² gives about 2.2 kPa at 60 m/s. Across one square kilometre, that is roughly 2.2×10⁹ N. Compared with about 200 tonnes of lift for a heavy airship, the static-force scale alone implies on the order of a thousand airships per square kilometre—before gusts, tethers, sheet tearing, or control authority. This is a Fermi comparison, not a design load case.

Even 100,000 heavy airships × ~200 tonnes gives ~20 million tonnes of total lift: only about 89 km² of sheet at that Cat-4 load, still roughly three orders of magnitude short of a regional drag field. A 20 km-long × 2 km-tall wall in 20 m/s tropical-storm winds would face roughly 10¹⁰ N, about one million tonnes-force. Splitting the sheet among different heights divides the hardware, not the total load.

Verdict: reject the airship-suspended sheet as storm hardware. Keep distributed drag as a real mechanism worth modeling in fixed, dual-use infrastructure. One hundred thousand airships solve every delivery problem in this report and none of the physics problems—the right fleet, if the job is delivery.

Published cousin

Offshore wind “speed bumps”

Jacobson, Archer, and Kempton modeled about 78,000 offshore turbines as a broad drag field and found lower peak winds and storm surge. That supports the mechanism, not the sheet architecture; turbines are fixed, distributed, and useful between storms. [18]

Various heights

The worst winds are where the wall must work

The hurricane-feeding inflow is concentrated in the lowest roughly 1–2 km. Higher sheets intercept less of that mass flow while making tether geometry and control harder. Sea anchoring only transfers the same load from airship to mooring.

Keep the airships

100,000 solve delivery, not physics

For spraying, seeding, and sensing ahead of the storm, 100,000 airships overwhelm the deployment problem: Salter’s cloud-brightening concept called for ~1,500 spray vessels, so this fleet is roughly 60× larger. [5] But at a wildly generous 10 MW of effective cooling each, the fleet totals 1 TW against a hurricane’s ~600 TW heat-release scale. [2] With station-keeping around 25–35 m/s versus 30–60+ m/s inflow, they still belong outside the strongest winds.

06 / Steering pivot

Do not dissipate it. Nudge its direction.

This is the smartest pivot in the report: steering changes the objective from removing a storm-sized heat budget to creating a smaller, deliberate asymmetry. HEAR-TS keep the dissipation job; steering becomes a separate control problem.

New binding constraintPrecision, not powera nudge can be smaller than the forecast error used to aim it

A small asymmetric patch could replace a giant symmetric one.

Weakening calls for the Hlywiak-scale cooled patch around the storm’s path. The steering hypothesis cools only one side of the forecast track: convection should weaken on the cooled side, making the vortex’s diabatic heating lopsided and biasing the center toward the warmer side. In principle that should need far less energy, but no coupled model yet establishes the required patch size, power, direction, or displacement.

Tropical cyclones still ride the deep-layer environmental flow and beta drift. Track research shows that convective asymmetries can feed back on motion, but this is a second-order effect. A hypothetical tens-of-kilometres deflection over days would be small beside the hundreds of kilometres per day imposed by the surrounding flow. No published study demonstrates a controlled, aimed track deflection from an artificial sea-surface-temperature asymmetry.

Verdict: conceptually, this is the cheapest objective in the set, and a 12.9 million-hull fleet offers distributed control capacity. But there is not yet enough evidence to claim its ~6.5 TW budget buys a specific nudge. Precision replaces energy as the likely binding constraint. It is a nudge, not a rudder.

Split the labor

Steering track: create and measure a small asymmetry ahead of the storm, with airships serving as sensing and delivery platforms outside the strongest winds.

Dissipation track: HEAR-TS retain the cooling, mixing, misting, and flux-reduction work. Their generous fleet-scale estimate is ~6.5 TW, but no steering study yet converts that power into a patch size or track change.

The aiming problem

Three-day track errors run roughly 100–200 km. If artificial asymmetry produced only a tens-of-kilometres nudge, the effect would be smaller than the uncertainty in the starting forecast. Sparing one city may require moving landfall by 100 km or more, reliably. The intervention cannot yet be aimed, verified, or corrected in time.

Open question: can coupled models produce a repeatable sign and magnitude of deflection?
Prior claim

Cool left or right

Abandoned application US20110101124A1 explicitly claims steering a hurricane by cooling air on the left or right of its projected path. It presents no demonstrated result. [19]

Prior claim

Redirect from cities

US8262314B2 explicitly claims redirecting storms away from populated areas. It is patent language, not field evidence. [20]

Maximum liability

Whoever steers owns the landfall

In 1965, Hurricane Betsy was considered for Project Stormfury but was not seeded; after its track turned toward Florida, public accusations still blamed the government. No international framework now decides who may redirect a storm or who answers for the coast it reaches. Dissipation that fails wastes effort; steering that “succeeds” chooses who bears the harm. [8] [14]

07 / Reality check

At 12.9 million hulls, scale stops being the first objection.

Hlywiak and Nolan still modeled the generous case: an ideal ocean, a perfect three-day track forecast, and cooling that appears instantly in exactly the right place. The fleet can reach the single-patch Fermi band; it cannot improve the weak modeled outcome.

Paper-reported heat extraction4.5×10¹⁹ kJ≈450× total US energy use in 2019, as published

Even the impossible best case only partly weakens the storm.

The largest 2°C cooled patch cut central pressure by at most ~15%; integrated kinetic energy changed less. Once the storm left the cooled zone, it could intensify again over warm water. The study’s conclusion was blunt: targeted artificial ocean cooling would be futile. [3]

Unit caution: the journal reports 4.5×10¹⁹ kJ and the 450× comparison. Those figures are difficult to reconcile with the published patch dimensions using a simple seawater heat-capacity check, so this page treats them as the paper’s estimate rather than an independently verified number.

This does not prove every flux-blocking or steering idea impossible. It does set the burden of proof: a proposal has to outperform idealized cooling, survive forecast error, and avoid moving risk somewhere else.

But 0.5 MW per hull is generous and Sirovich’s COP is only a thermodynamic minimum; worse efficiency can erase the margin. Full-season maintenance over 5–10 million km² is roughly 20–40 patches and about an order of magnitude beyond the fleet on midpoint assumptions. [7]

08 / Surface covers

Blankets attack evaporation directly — then lose on mass and survival.

A cover could suppress the dominant latent-heat flux without cooling the full water column. That is the cleanest physics case for including blankets. It is not a clean deployment case.

What works in principle

Floating-disc reservoir experiments cut evaporation by about 80%. A 2023 patent proposes reflective or insulating floating objects held in boom-like enclosures with anchors, moorings, dynamic positioning, and tugs. Bench tests reported over 98% coverage and ~2.5× lower evaporation. [9] [10]

  • Evaporation suppression throttles water-vapor supply.
  • Reflective shading can slow solar heating.
  • Boat-compatible gear means spools, booms, towing, and station-keeping.

What kills storm-scale deployment

A 100 μm HDPE film over 260,000 km² weighs roughly 25 million tonnes — about 6% of one year’s global plastic production. A 50 m hull carrying 20 t of film covers ~200,000 m²; one patch takes roughly 1.2 million hull-loads. These are report extrapolations, not field measurements.

  • Open-ocean survival at 60+ m/s winds and 15+ m seas is unproven.
  • Deep-water anchoring fails in 4,000+ m genesis regions.
  • Shredding turns the system into basin-scale macro- and microplastic debris.

Monolayer alcohol or oil films can reduce evaporation, but wind piles them downwind within hours — a fatal weakness in hurricane conditions. Coastal floating solar has survived severe typhoon winds, but no untethered open-ocean cover has equivalent evidence. [11]

09 / Heat options

Heating the core adds fuel. Peripheral heating is only a hypothesis.

“Heat or cool” is not symmetric. Where the heat goes decides whether it feeds the engine or perturbs the circulation around it.

Under or ahead of the core

Warmer water raises evaporation and available enthalpy. Heating the track would tend to strengthen the storm, especially where deep ocean heat content is already high.

Verdict: counterproductive.

At the periphery

A 2024 workshop abstract by Li, Chakraborty, and Miyoshi reports that artificially heating a typhoon’s periphery can significantly diminish intensity “with reasonable cost.” The mechanism, energy requirement, and peer-reviewed paper were not available.

Verdict: model first; no hardware assumptions yet. [12]
10 / Hull fit

What a 50 m trimaran can honestly do.

Hydrogen supplies stored energy; it does not generate it. Deck solar contributes only tens of kilowatts on average, and a hull-scale wave converter perhaps hundreds in a good wave climate.

One hull0.1–0.5 MW

Generous mission-payload budget from deck solar, wave capture, and stored hydrogen. The upper value is the assumption behind the fleet-scale correction.

12.9 million hulls~6.5 TW

Enough to clear the 0.3–3 TW Fermi band for one idealized patch. If delivered power or mixing efficiency misses the generous assumptions, the margin can vanish.

Per-hull assignment~19 m³/s

For the full 260,000 km² × ~80 m volume over 24 hours, the flow per hull becomes recognizable dredge-pump scale. The hard question shifts from flow to whether the intervention buys enough weakening.

11 / Consequences

Weakening a storm is not the same as reducing harm.

Intervention changes ocean ecology, rainfall, track, and legal liability. A weaker but wetter or redirected storm may still create equal or greater damage.

The steering problem is a governance problem.If cooling shifts landfall from one coast to another, the operator has redistributed risk across borders without a liability framework.
  • 01Bloom and oxygen risk. Nutrient- and carbon-rich deep water can trigger phytoplankton blooms, harmful algae, oxygen drawdown, and food-web shifts. Outcomes are nonlinear and poorly understood. [13]
  • 02Greenhouse-gas release. Upwelled water can outgas dissolved CO₂, methane, and nitrous oxide.
  • 03Rainfall redistribution. Changed sea-surface gradients move convection; geoengineering benefits are spatially uneven.
  • 04Blanket ecology. Opaque cover blocks phytoplankton light, suppresses oxygen and carbon-dioxide exchange, and can create storm debris.
  • 05Legal vacuum. ENMOD bans hostile environmental modification, not peaceful hurricane suppression. No international regulator or liability rule covers a cross-border intervention. [14]
12 / Honest holes

What has to be disproved before this becomes engineering.

The next step is not a fleet build. It is a small number of experiments designed to kill the idea quickly if the physics, ecology, or governance cannot survive contact with reality.

The central dealbreaker

No proponent has published a rebuttal to Hlywiak’s result: even perfect, instant, free cooling across a massive patch barely weakens the storm. Every cooling method inherits that ceiling until a coupled model shows otherwise.

No meaningful field validation

Bulk seawater misting, airship sheet windbreaks, bubble curtains, Salter Sinks, powered upwelling, marine cloud brightening, and storm blankets have not been tested at hurricane-relevant scale. Bench, ground-spray, and reservoir evidence does not bridge to a storm-sized ocean patch.

Unknown mixing efficiency

Sirovich’s COP near 10⁴ is a thermodynamic minimum. Real turbulent mixing in currents, eddies, and a stratified ocean could be 10–1,000× worse. No large experiment has measured it.

Forecast dependence

The key model assumes a perfect forecast three days ahead. Real three-day track errors around 100–200 km are comparable to the tested patch widths; a misplaced patch can waste the whole intervention.

Where does the storm’s energy go?

If the system prevents or weakens a storm, coupled models need to show what happens to the heat and moisture the storm would have moved. Does the climate self-correct as if no storm happened, or must the intervention move that energy elsewhere itself? Track ocean heat storage, currents, rainfall, and later storms—not only the treated storm.

Methods to stop carrying

Drop per-storm blanket deployment, airship-suspended sheet windbreaks, monolayer films in high wind, heating under the core, Kitamura-scale submarine pumping, and nuclear or explosive options. Keep peripheral heating and fixed distributed drag as modeling questions only.

13 / Research gate

Four tests before anything larger.

Independent model comparison

Reproduce or falsify Hlywiak across multiple coupled ocean–atmosphere models, including flux-blocking blankets, peripheral heating, staged 0.1°C annual changes, and the downstream energy balance when storms are prevented or weakened.

≥10 km field trial

Compare warm-surface mist, deep-cold mist, direct mixing, and an unsprayed control. Measure upper-ocean heat, air humidity, droplet drift, and net enthalpy change per unit energy.

Ecology ladder

Move sustained upwelling from mesocosm to bounded field testing; measure oxygen, harmful algae, carbon outgassing, and food-web change.

Authority before deployment

Define who may intervene, who consents, who bears redirected-storm liability, and what automatically stops an experiment.
